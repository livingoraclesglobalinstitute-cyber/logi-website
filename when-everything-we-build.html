When Everything We Build Must Eventually Be Left Behind: Finding Meaning in a Temporary Life

Prophet Ing. Stephen Adusei
Reading Time: 45 minutes • August 21, 2026

This book is a Christian reflection, personal testimony, and spiritual encouragement. It confronts the reality that everything we build in this earthly life—possessions, achievements, reputations, and even our physical bodies—will eventually be left behind. Drawing from the author's personal experience of losing both parents, this work explores the deep questions that arise when we face the temporary nature of life: Why are we here? What truly matters? What remains when everything we have built is left behind? Through biblical exposition, personal narrative, and theological reflection, the author guides readers through the valleys of grief, the wisdom of Ecclesiastes, the hope of resurrection, and the call to faithful stewardship. The book argues that temporary does not mean meaningless—rather, the brevity of life makes our choices sacred. We are called to live intentionally, love deeply, serve faithfully, and invest in what has eternal significance. The central message is that we cannot keep everything we build, but we can faithfully deposit something eternal into the lives of others. This work includes reflection questions for personal journaling or group discussion, along with prayers for perspective, grief, legacy, stewardship, hope, and commitment. It is an invitation to discover that our temporary life has an eternal purpose.

Foreword

This book was born from silence. Not the silence of emptiness, but the silence of deep thought—the kind that settles over you when life becomes too heavy for words.

There are moments in every person's life when the noise of daily existence fades away, and we are left staring at the bigger questions: Why am I here? What does any of this mean? If everything I build will eventually be left behind, why build at all?

These are not academic questions. They are the questions that rise from the depths of human experience—from loss, from struggle, from the quiet hours of the night when we cannot escape our own thoughts.

I write this book not as someone who has all the answers, but as someone who has walked through the valley of these questions and emerged with a different perspective.

I lost my parents. I watched them work, struggle, provide, and eventually leave everything behind. Their deaths forced me to confront something I had previously only understood intellectually: life is temporary.

But here is what I have come to discover: Temporary does not mean meaningless. And the fact that everything we build will eventually be left behind does not make building worthless—it makes building sacred.

This book is an invitation to think deeply about your life. To ask hard questions. To sit with the discomfort of uncertainty. And ultimately, to discover that meaning is not found in permanence, but in faithfulness.

What You Will Find in This Book

Each chapter weaves together personal reflection with the timeless wisdom of Scripture. You will encounter:

  • The words of the King James Bible speaking directly to each theme
  • Biblical characters who faced similar struggles
  • Practical applications for your daily life
  • Questions to help you reflect and grow

But most of all, you will find my journey—the story of a son who lost his parents and discovered that the purpose of a temporary life is not to possess what cannot be kept, but to faithfully deposit something eternal into the lives of others.

Whether you are grieving, questioning, or simply searching for something more than the endless cycle of work and consumption, I hope these pages offer you something valuable.

Let us begin.

1. The Quiet Moments That Change Everything

There are moments when I become quiet—not because I have nothing to say, but because I am thinking deeply about life.

Perhaps you know these moments. They come unexpectedly. Maybe in the middle of a busy day, while driving, while sitting alone, or in the stillness of the night when sleep will not come. Suddenly, the noise of the world fades, and you are left face to face with the big questions.

I sometimes look at this world and wonder: What am I really living for?

It is a question that has haunted humanity for thousands of years. Philosophers have pondered it. Poets have written about it. Religious leaders have taught it. And yet, each person must eventually answer it for themselves.

I remember sitting alone after my mother's funeral. The house was quiet. Her chair was empty. Her belongings were still there—her clothes, her dishes, her Bible—but she was gone.

And I thought: She worked so hard for all of this. And now, she cannot take any of it with her.

That thought would not leave me.

We wake up every morning and begin again. We work for food. We work for a shelter. We struggle to provide for our families. We pursue education, build careers, establish businesses, serve in ministry, acquire possessions, make plans for the future, and try to leave something behind.

And then, one day, everything stops. We leave it all behind.

This is not pessimism. This is reality. And the confrontation with that reality—if we allow it—can change everything about how we live.

The Wisdom of the Psalmist

David understood this feeling well. He cried out to the Lord:

"LORD, make me to know mine end, and the measure of my days, what it is; that I may know how frail I am. Behold, thou hast made my days as an handbreadth; and mine age is as nothing before thee: verily every man at his best state is altogether vanity." — Psalm 39:4-5

David did not ask to know the number of his days out of morbid curiosity. He asked so that he might live wisely. He understood that life is brief—a mere handbreadth, a breath.

Moses also wrote:

"The days of our years are threescore years and ten; and if by reason of strength they be fourscore years, yet is their strength labour and sorrow; for it is soon cut off, and we fly away." — Psalm 90:10

But Moses did not stop there. He prayed:

"So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom." — Psalm 90:12

This is the key. The purpose of recognizing life's shortness is not despair—it is wisdom. When you know that your time is limited, you begin to prioritize what truly matters.

The Lord Jesus Himself taught:

"Take therefore no thought for the morrow: for the morrow shall take thought for the things of itself. Sufficient unto the day is the evil thereof." — Matthew 6:34

We are not to live in anxiety about tomorrow, but we are to live with the awareness that today is a gift.

2. What Am I Really Living For?

The question lingers: What am I really living for?

Think about your daily life. How much of your time is spent on things that will not matter in ten years? In twenty years? In eternity?

We spend hours working to earn money to buy things that will eventually break, wear out, or be left behind. We worry about our reputations, our status, our possessions—all of which will fade. We plan for a future we cannot guarantee. We invest in things that cannot accompany us beyond this life.

This is not to say that work, planning, and provision are wrong. They are necessary. They are part of being human. But the question is: What are they ultimately for?

If everything we build will eventually be left behind, then what is the point?

I remember my mother and my father. They lived. They struggled. They worked. They endured difficulties. They tried to survive and provide. They had their hopes, their responsibilities, their dreams, and their worries. But eventually, they died.

And when they died, they could not take their possessions with them. They could not take their houses. They could not take their money. They could not take their achievements. They could not take the things they spent years working to obtain.

They left everything behind.

Watching that reality has caused me to think deeply about my own life. Sometimes I ask myself: What is the meaning of all this? Why do we struggle so much if one day we will leave everything behind? Why do we spend so much energy fighting for things that cannot follow us into eternity? Why does life seem so full of suffering, labour, disappointment and uncertainty, only for death to eventually remind us that we were never going to keep anything forever?

These are not easy questions. And I don't pretend to have simple answers. But I have found that asking them—honestly, persistently—has led me to a deeper understanding of what truly matters.

The Words of Jesus About Treasure

"Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal: but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt, and where thieves do not break through nor steal: for where your treasure is, there will your heart be also." — Matthew 6:19-21

Notice the contrast the Lord makes: Earthly treasures are temporary. They can be destroyed, stolen, or lost. Heavenly treasures are eternal. They cannot be taken away.

Jesus is not saying we should not work or provide for our families. He is saying we should not make earthly treasures our ultimate purpose. He continues:

"No man can serve two masters: for either he will hate the one, and love the other; or else he will hold to the one, and despise the other. Ye cannot serve God and mammon." — Matthew 6:24

This is a warning about where we place our ultimate loyalty. We cannot give our hearts to both God and wealth.

The apostle Paul also wrote:

"Charge them that are rich in this world, that they be not high-minded, nor trust in uncertain riches, but in the living God, who giveth us richly all things to enjoy." — 1 Timothy 6:17

We can enjoy the things God gives us, but we must not put our hope in them. They are uncertain. God is not.

Solomon, the wisest man who ever lived, also warned:

"He that trusteth in his riches shall fall: but the righteous shall flourish as a branch." — Proverbs 11:28

3. The Weight of Loss

Loss changes you. It changes the way you see the world. It changes the way you see time. It changes the way you see relationships and possessions and plans.

Before I lost my parents, I understood death conceptually. I knew it happened to everyone. I had attended funerals. I had sympathized with others who had lost loved ones. But understanding something intellectually is not the same as experiencing it personally.

When my parents died, I entered a place I had never been before. A place of deep grief. A place of silence. A place where the normal rhythms of life felt strange and meaningless.

I remember thinking: They are gone. They are just... gone.

The meals they used to eat. The chair where my father used to sit. The voice I would hear on the phone. The presence I had known my entire life—all of it was suddenly absent.

And the things they had worked for—their house, their possessions, their achievements—all of it remained, but they could no longer enjoy any of it.

Death stopped being an idea. It became personal. It became a memory. It became a reality I could not ignore.

I remember walking through their house after they were gone. Everything was exactly as they had left it. My mother's Bible was still open on the table. My father's shoes were still by the door.

I stood there and wept. Not because I was sad about the things—the things did not matter. I wept because the people who owned them were gone. I wept because I would never hear their voices again. I wept because I had so many questions I would never get to ask them.

Grief has a way of stripping away the non-essentials. When you are grieving, you stop caring about trivial things. Status no longer matters. The latest trends no longer matter. What other people think no longer matters.

What matters is love. What matters is presence. What matters is the things that cannot be taken away. And strangely, grief brought me closer to God.

The Tears of Jesus

One of the most powerful passages in Scripture about grief is the story of Lazarus.

"When Jesus therefore saw her weeping, and the Jews also weeping which came with her, he groaned in the spirit, and was troubled, and said, Where have ye laid him? They said unto him, Lord, come and see. Jesus wept." — John 11:33-35

Jesus wept. The Son of God, who knew he was about to raise Lazarus from the dead, still wept. He did not weep because he lacked faith. He wept because he loved. He wept because death is painful. He wept because grief is real.

This is important for us to understand: Grief is not a failure of faith. Even Jesus grieved. Even Jesus wept. Even Jesus was troubled in spirit.

Solomon wrote:

"To every thing there is a season, and a time to every purpose under the heaven: a time to weep, and a time to laugh; a time to mourn, and a time to dance." — Ecclesiastes 3:1,4

Mourning is not discouraged in Scripture. It is expected. It is part of being human.

The apostle Paul wrote:

"Rejoice with them that do rejoice, and weep with them that weep." — Romans 12:15

We are called to enter into one another's grief, not to dismiss it.

A Moment to Reflect

Have you given yourself permission to grieve? Have you allowed yourself to feel the weight of loss, or have you tried to move past it too quickly?

4. Grief and the Nearness of God

I did not expect grief to bring me closer to God. If anything, I might have expected the opposite. I might have expected anger. I might have expected doubt. I might have expected to walk away from faith entirely.

But instead, I found myself turning toward God. I asked questions. I prayed. I became quieter. I began looking beyond the things that occupy our attention every day.

There are things you can understand intellectually, but there are other things you only understand when life forces you to experience them.

Before losing my parents, I knew that life was temporary. After losing them, I felt it. And in that feeling, something shifted.

I began to understand that perhaps the greatest problem is not that life is temporary. Perhaps the problem is that we often expect temporary things to give us permanent meaning.

We look to money to give us security. We look to relationships to give us fulfillment. We look to achievements to give us purpose. We look to possessions to give us comfort.

But all of these things are temporary. They were never designed to carry the weight of eternity. And when we place that weight on them, they break.

Grief taught me to stop expecting temporary things to provide permanent meaning.

The Nearness of God in Suffering

"The LORD is nigh unto them that are of a broken heart; and saveth such as be of a contrite spirit." — Psalm 34:18
"He healeth the broken in heart, and bindeth up their wounds." — Psalm 147:3
"My flesh and my heart faileth: but God is the strength of my heart, and my portion for ever." — Psalm 73:26

Notice that the Bible does not say God removes all suffering. It says He is near to the brokenhearted. He heals the brokenhearted. He is our strength when our own strength fails.

The apostle Paul wrote about his own suffering:

"For this thing I besought the Lord thrice, that it might depart from me. And he said unto me, My grace is sufficient for thee: for my strength is made perfect in weakness." — 2 Corinthians 12:8-9

Paul did not get what he asked for. But he got something better: he got God's grace and power in his weakness.

When we are weak, God's strength is made perfect in us. When we are broken, God draws near to us.

Isaiah prophesied of the suffering Messiah:

"He is despised and rejected of men; a man of sorrows, and acquainted with grief: and we hid as it were our faces from him; he was despised, and we esteemed him not. Surely he hath borne our griefs, and carried our sorrows." — Isaiah 53:3-4

Jesus Himself is described as "a man of sorrows, acquainted with grief." He understands our pain because He experienced it.

A Moment to Reflect

In what ways has grief drawn you closer to God? Have you experienced His nearness in your pain?

5. What Ecclesiastes Understood

One of the books of the Bible that speaks to me deeply in this area is Ecclesiastes.

The writer looks at human labour, wisdom, wealth, pleasure and achievement and asks what lasting gain a person receives from all their work.

"Vanity of vanities, saith the Preacher, vanity of vanities; all is vanity. What profit hath a man of all his labour which he taketh under the sun?" — Ecclesiastes 1:2-3
"One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh: but the earth abideth for ever." — Ecclesiastes 1:4

When I first read these words after my parents died, they struck me with a force I had never felt before. One generation passeth away, and another generation cometh. That was my parents. They were the generation that passed away. And I was the generation that remained.

The Preacher observes what every generation eventually discovers: We work, we build, we accumulate, and eventually another generation takes over. That reality can make life seem empty.

But Ecclesiastes does not simply teach us to give up. Instead, it exposes the danger of trying to find ultimate meaning in things that were never designed to carry the weight of eternity.

Money is temporary. Fame is temporary. Possessions are temporary. Positions are temporary. Even our physical bodies are temporary.

If we make these things our ultimate purpose, eventually we will become disappointed. Because they cannot satisfy the deepest questions of the human soul.

Ecclesiastes teaches us to see life clearly. To recognize its brevity. To acknowledge the limitations of human effort. And then, to find meaning not in the things we accumulate, but in the things that endure beyond this life.

The writer concludes:

"Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man." — Ecclesiastes 12:13

Not accumulating. Not achieving. Not impressive. Fearing God. Obeying God. Living in a relationship with the One who is eternal. That is where lasting meaning is found.

What the Preacher Explored

Solomon explores various pursuits to see if any provide lasting meaning:

"What hath man of all his labour, and of the vexation of his heart, wherein he hath laboured under the sun?" — Ecclesiastes 2:22

The conclusion is clear: Nothing in this earthly life—viewed by itself—provides ultimate meaning. When we look at life without God, it does seem meaningless. But when we look at life with God—from an eternal perspective—everything changes.

A Moment to Reflect

What earthly pursuits have you been relying on for meaning? How does adding God to the equation change your perspective?

6. My Parents Did Not Live Meaningless Lives

Sometimes when I think about my parents, I could look at their lives and say: They struggled, they survived, and then they died. So what was the point?

But I have come to see it differently. A life does not become worthless because it has an ending.

I remember my mother's hands. They were worn from years of work. She cooked, she cleaned, she sewed, she held us when we were sick. Those hands touched my face when I was a child. Those hands prayed over me.

I remember my father's voice. He was not a man of many words, but when he spoke, you listened. He taught me to work hard. He taught me to provide for my family. He taught me to endure difficulty without complaining.

My parents gave something to me that cannot be measured by money. They gave me life. They influenced me. They endured things I may never fully understand. They left memories. They left lessons. They left values.

And, whether they realized it or not, they became part of the person I am becoming.

Perhaps this is one of the great mysteries of human existence: We may leave the world, but what we deposited into other people can continue after we are gone.

A parent may die, but what they taught their child may continue for generations. A teacher may die, but the knowledge they imparted may influence thousands of people. A minister may die, but the lives they touched may continue touching others. A person may leave this earth, but their influence can continue moving through people they encountered.

That makes me think differently about legacy.

The Legacy of the Faithful

Scripture is filled with examples of people whose influence continued long after they died.

Abraham left a legacy of faith that continues to this day. God promised him:

"I will make of thee a great nation, and I will bless thee, and make thy name great; and thou shalt be a blessing: and I will bless them that bless thee, and curse him that curseth thee: and in thee shall all families of the earth be blessed." — Genesis 12:2-3

Paul poured his life into others. He wrote to Timothy:

"Thou therefore, my son, be strong in the grace that is in Christ Jesus. And the things that thou hast heard of me among many witnesses, the same commit thou to faithful men, who shall be able to teach others also." — 2 Timothy 2:1-2

Notice the chain Paul describes: Paul taught Timothy. Timothy would teach faithful men. Those men would teach others. Four generations of influence.

The writer of Hebrews describes the legacy of faith:

"Wherefore seeing we also are compassed about with so great a cloud of witnesses, let us lay aside every weight, and the sin which doth so easily beset us, and let us run with patience the race that is set before us." — Hebrews 12:1

Those who have gone before us are witnesses to the faithfulness of God. Their lives speak to us. Their example encourages us. And Paul assured the Corinthians:

"Therefore, my beloved brethren, be ye stedfast, unmoveable, always abounding in the work of the Lord, forasmuch as ye know that your labour is not in vain in the Lord." — 1 Corinthians 15:58

7. What I Must Deposit in Others

If my parents deposited something in me, then I must deposit something in others. This is not optional. It is the nature of human existence. We all leave something behind. The only question is: What will it be?

I want to leave more than material things. I want to leave faith. I want to leave love. I want to leave integrity. I want to leave wisdom. I want to leave a life that points others to God. I want to leave people better than I found them. I want to raise people who know God.

I want to teach. I want to serve. I want to build things that help others. I want to use whatever God has placed in my hands faithfully.

When my own time comes, I don't want my greatest concern to be how much I accumulated. I want to be able to look back and say: I was faithful with what I was given.

The Example of Barnabas

Barnabas was known as the "Son of Encouragement." He invested in Paul when others doubted him. He invested in Mark when others rejected him. His legacy was not buildings or money—it was people.

"And let us not be weary in doing well: for in due season we shall reap, if we faint not. As we have therefore opportunity, let us do good unto all men, especially unto them who are of the household of faith." — Galatians 6:9-10

Paul did not just preach to the Thessalonians—he imparted his very soul to them. He gave them not just a message, but his life. (1 Thessalonians 2:8)

"He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing." — John 15:5

8. Stewardship, Not Ownership

Perhaps we were never meant to keep everything. Perhaps we were meant to steward what God places in our hands for the time we are here.

We don't own tomorrow. We don't own our possessions permanently. We don't even own our time. We receive these things as gifts and responsibilities. And then eventually, we hand them back.

This changes the way I think about life. Instead of asking only: "What can I get?" I begin asking: "What can I give?" Instead of asking: "How much can I accomplish before I die?" I ask: "Was I faithful with what God gave me?"

Those are very different questions. One set of questions is focused on accumulating. The other is focused on stewarding. One set is focused on what I can keep. The other is focused on what I can invest. One set is focused on temporary gain. The other is focused on eternal significance.

When you see yourself as a steward rather than an owner, everything changes. You stop clinging. You start giving. You stop worrying. You start trusting. You stop grasping. You start releasing.

You realize that everything you have is a loan from God—and you will eventually give an account for how you used it.

Stewardship in Scripture

"For the kingdom of heaven is as a man travelling into a far country, who called his own servants, and delivered unto them his goods. And unto one he gave five talents, to another two, and to another one; to every man according to his several abilities." — Matthew 25:14-15

When the master returned, he asked for an account. The faithful servants were rewarded; the unfaithful servant was condemned. The key is this: Everything belongs to the master. The servants are responsible for what they have been given.

The Parable of the Rich Fool

"The ground of a certain rich man brought forth plentifully: and he thought within himself, saying, What shall I do, because I have no room where to bestow my fruits? And he said, This will I do: I will pull down my barns, and build greater; and there I will bestow all my fruits and my goods. And I will say to my soul, Soul, thou hast much goods laid up for many years; take thine ease, eat, drink, and be merry. But God said unto him, Thou fool, this night thy soul shall be required of thee: then whose shall those things be, which thou hast provided? So is he that layeth up treasure for himself, and is not rich toward God." — Luke 12:16-21

The rich fool thought he owned his wealth. But he was only a steward, and his time as steward ended suddenly.

God's Ownership

"Behold, the heaven and the heaven of heavens is the LORD's thy God, the earth also, with all that therein is." — Deuteronomy 10:14
"The earth is the LORD's, and the fulness thereof; the world, and they that dwell therein." — Psalm 24:1
"For every beast of the forest is mine, and the cattle upon a thousand hills." — Psalm 50:10
"As every man hath received the gift, even so minister the same one to another, as good stewards of the manifold grace of God." — 1 Peter 4:10

9. Honest Lament and Faith

I have sometimes thought about this from the perspective of ministry. I am a prophet. I preach. I teach. I speak about God. I encourage people to have faith. But being called by God does not mean that I stop being human. It does not mean I never become tired. It does not mean I never ask difficult questions. It does not mean I never sit quietly and wonder about the meaning of everything.

There have been nights when I have lain awake, staring at the ceiling, and thought: I preach about eternity. I tell people that life is temporary and that they should live for what lasts. But tonight, I am struggling with the same questions they struggle with. I am tired. I am grieving. I am still missing my parents. And sometimes, I wonder if any of what I do really matters.

I don't say this to discourage anyone. I say it because I think there is something important in admitting it. Faith is not pretending that we have no questions. Faith is being willing to bring our questions into the presence of God.

There have been people in Scripture who loved God deeply and yet experienced seasons of tremendous emotional and spiritual exhaustion. Jeremiah wept and wished he had never been born. David poured out his heart in lament, crying, "My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me?" Job questioned God and demanded an answer. Elijah, after a great victory, was so depressed that he wanted to die. Jesus Himself, in the garden of Gethsemane, sweated drops of blood and asked if there was another way.

Having questions does not mean you lack faith. It means you are human.

Honest Lament in Scripture

"My God, my God, why hast thou forsaken me? why art thou so far from helping me, and from the words of my roaring? O my God, I cry in the daytime, but thou hearest not; and in the night season, and am not silent." — Psalm 22:1-2
"How long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? for ever? how long wilt thou hide thy face from me? How long shall I take counsel in my soul, having sorrow in my heart daily?" — Psalm 13:1-2
"I am weary with my groaning; all the night make I my bed to swim; I water my couch with my tears." — Psalm 6:6
"Cursed be the day wherein I was born: let not the day wherein my mother bare me be blessed." — Jeremiah 20:14
"Why died I not from the womb? why did I not give up the ghost when I came out of the belly?... I was not in safety, neither had I rest, neither was I quiet; yet trouble came." — Job 3:11,26
"And he taketh with him Peter and James and John, and began to be sore amazed, and to be very heavy; and saith unto them, My soul is exceeding sorrowful unto death." — Mark 14:33-34
"Saying, Father, if thou be willing, remove this cup from me: nevertheless not my will, but thine, be done." — Luke 22:42

Even the Lord Jesus experienced deep distress. He asked if there was another way. He was honest about His suffering.

A Moment to Reflect

Are you being honest with God about your questions and struggles? Do you believe He can handle your honesty?

10. The Temporary Can Still Be Precious

One thing I am learning is that something does not have to last forever to have meaning.

A conversation with someone we love may last an hour. A child's childhood lasts only a few years. A beautiful sunset lasts minutes. A meal disappears. A season passes. A person's earthly life eventually ends.

Yet none of these things are meaningless simply because they are temporary. In fact, perhaps their temporary nature makes them more precious.

I remember sitting with my mother in her final weeks. We did not talk about anything important, really. We talked about ordinary things—the weather, the food, the memories. But those conversations were precious. They were precious precisely because they would not last forever.

If I had known I would have unlimited time with my mother, would I have treasured those moments the same way? Probably not.

It is the very brevity of life that gives it weight. It is the fact that we have only so much time that makes time precious.

When I think about my parents now, I don't want only to think about the fact that they died. I want to remember where they lived. They were here. They loved. They struggled. They endured. They contributed something to this world. And for a period of time, their lives intersected with mine. That matters.

Redeeming the Time

"See then that ye walk circumspectly, not as fools, but as wise, redeeming the time, because the days are evil." — Ephesians 5:15-16
"Walk in wisdom toward them that are without, redeeming the time." — Colossians 4:5
"Whereas ye know not what shall be on the morrow." — James 4:14
"Boast not thyself of to morrow; for thou knowest not what a day may bring forth." — Proverbs 27:1

Because life is short and uncertain, we should use our time wisely—especially in love, service, and faithfulness to God.

A Moment to Reflect

What temporary things in your life are actually precious? How can you treasure them more fully today?

11. Since I Will Die, How Should I Live?

Maybe the question is not: "Why should I live when I will eventually die?" Perhaps the better question is: "Since I will eventually die, how should I live?"

That question changes everything.

If my life is temporary, then I should love while I have the opportunity. I should forgive while I have the opportunity. I should serve while I have the opportunity. I should speak the truth while I have the opportunity. I should encourage people while I have the opportunity.

I should build people, not merely things. I should pursue God while I have breath. I should use my gifts while I have time. I should not spend my entire life chasing things that cannot accompany me into eternity.

When my parents died, I realized something: they did not die regretting that they had not accumulated more. They died with other concerns—concerns about their children, about their faith, about the things that truly mattered.

I want to live with those same concerns. I don't want to wake up one day and realize that I spent my entire life chasing things that could not follow me into eternity.

"For I am now ready to be offered, and the time of my departure is at hand. I have fought a good fight, I have finished my course, I have kept the faith: Henceforth there is laid up for me a crown of righteousness, which the Lord, the righteous judge, shall give me at that day: and not to me only, but unto all them also that love his appearing." — 2 Timothy 4:6-8

Paul lived with the finish line in view. He knew his time was limited, and he used it faithfully.

The Call to Watchfulness

"Watch therefore: for ye know not what hour your Lord doth come." — Matthew 24:42
"Watch ye therefore, and pray always, that ye may be accounted worthy to escape all these things that shall come to pass, and to stand before the Son of man." — Luke 21:36
"Blessed is that servant, whom his lord when he cometh shall find so doing." — Matthew 24:46
"Whatsoever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might; for there is no work, nor device, nor knowledge, nor wisdom, in the grave, whither thou goest." — Ecclesiastes 9:10
"Not slothful in business; fervent in spirit; serving the Lord." — Romans 12:11

12. What Death Has Taught Me

The death of my parents has taught me something that no classroom could teach me. Life is short. But that does not mean life is worthless. It means life is valuable.

It means time matters. It means relationships matter. It means obedience matters. It means what we do with our days matters. And above all, it means eternity matters.

I don't want to live merely to survive. I don't want my entire existence to become a cycle of eating, working, paying bills, acquiring things and eventually dying.

I want my life to mean something beyond myself. I want to leave people better than I found them. I want to raise people who know God. I want to teach. I want to serve. I want to build things that help others. I want to use whatever God has placed in my hands faithfully.

And when my own time comes, I don't want my greatest concern to be how much I accumulated. I want to be able to look back and say: I was faithful with what I was given.

13. The Certainty of Death and the Call to Wisdom

"And as it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment." — Hebrews 9:27

There is no escape from death. It is appointed. It is certain. But notice what follows: judgment. Accountability.

"For we shall all stand before the judgment seat of Christ." — Romans 14:10

The reality of judgment should not terrify us if we are in Christ. But it should sober us. It should cause us to live carefully.

"The living know that they shall die: but the dead know not any thing, neither have they any more a reward; for the memory of them is forgotten. Also their love, and their hatred, and their envy, is now perished; neither have they any more a portion for ever in any thing that is done under the sun." — Ecclesiastes 9:5-6

This is a sobering picture of life without God. But for the believer, death is not the end—it is a transition.

"Precious in the sight of the LORD is the death of his saints." — Psalm 116:15
"The fear of the LORD is the beginning of wisdom: and the knowledge of the holy is understanding." — Proverbs 9:10
"So teach us to number our days, that we may apply our hearts unto wisdom." — Psalm 90:12

14. Hope Beyond the Grave

For me, this is where my faith becomes essential. If this life were all there is, then perhaps the struggle really would be difficult to explain. We would be born, struggle, experience moments of joy and pain, and eventually disappear.

But I believe that is not the whole story. I believe in God. I believe in Christ. I believe in the resurrection. I believe that death does not have the final word.

And because I believe in eternity, I can look at the temporary nature of this world without allowing it to completely destroy my hope.

My parents left this earthly world. One day, I will leave it too. Everyone I love will eventually leave it. But Christianity teaches me that the grave is not the end of God's story.

"I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me, though he were dead, yet shall he live: and whosoever liveth and believeth in me shall never die." — John 11:25-26
"Let not your heart be troubled: ye believe in God, believe also in me. In my Father's house are many mansions: if it were not so, I would have told you. I go to prepare a place for you. And if I go and prepare a place for you, I will come again, and receive you unto myself; that where I am, there ye may be also." — John 14:1-3
"For this corruptible must put on incorruption, and this mortal must put on immortality. So when this corruptible shall have put on incorruption, and this mortal shall have put on immortality, then shall be brought to pass the saying that is written, Death is swallowed up in victory. O death, where is thy sting? O grave, where is thy victory?" — 1 Corinthians 15:53-55
"For to me to live is Christ, and to die is gain." — Philippians 1:21
"We are confident, I say, and willing rather to be absent from the body, and to be present with the Lord." — 2 Corinthians 5:8
"Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, which according to his abundant mercy hath begotten us again unto a lively hope by the resurrection of Jesus Christ from the dead, to an inheritance incorruptible, and undefiled, and that fadeth not away, reserved in heaven for you." — 1 Peter 1:3-4
"And I heard a great voice out of heaven saying, Behold, the tabernacle of God is with men, and he will dwell with them, and they shall be his people, and God himself shall be with them, and be their God. And God shall wipe away all tears from their eyes; and there shall be no more death, neither sorrow, nor crying, neither shall there be any more pain: for the former things are passed away." — Revelation 21:3-4

A Moment to Reflect

How does the hope of resurrection change the way you view death? How does it change the way you view life?

15. What Am I Really Living For? (Conclusion)

So, what am I really living for?

I have thought about this question for years now. I have sat in silence. I have wept. I have prayed. I have searched the Scriptures. And here is what I have come to believe:

I am not here merely to eat. I am not here merely to work. I am not here merely to accumulate. I am not here merely to survive.

I am here to love. To serve. To learn. To grow. To give. To glorify God. To touch lives. To prepare for eternity.

And perhaps, when I finally leave this world, the greatest question will not be: "How much did you own?" But rather: "What did you do with the life you were given?"

That is the question I am learning to live with.

My parents could not take everything with them. Neither will I. And neither will you.

But while we are still here, we have something incredibly valuable: Time.

So I want to use mine well. I want to love deeply. I want to serve faithfully. I want to walk with God. I want to help people. I want to leave something meaningful in the hearts of others.

And when my earthly journey finally comes to an end, I pray that I will not have simply existed. I pray that I will have lived faithfully.

Because perhaps the meaning of this temporary life is not found in making ourselves permanent. Perhaps it is found in faithfully using our temporary lives for an eternal purpose.

"And thou shalt love the LORD thy God with all thine heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy might." — Deuteronomy 6:5
"Jesus said unto him, Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with all thy heart, and with all thy soul, and with all thy mind. This is the first and great commandment. And the second is like unto it, Thou shalt love thy neighbour as thyself." — Matthew 22:37-39
"I am the vine, ye are the branches: He that abideth in me, and I in him, the same bringeth forth much fruit: for without me ye can do nothing." — John 15:5
"Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth... but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven." — Matthew 6:19-20
"Set your affection on things above, not on things on the earth." — Colossians 3:2
"Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man." — Ecclesiastes 12:13
"Be thou faithful unto death, and I will give thee a crown of life." — Revelation 2:10

A Moment to Reflect

If you were to answer the question "What are you really living for?" with complete honesty, what would you say?

Conclusion: Temporary Does Not Mean Meaningless

We are all builders. We build careers. We build families. We build homes. We build reputations. We build dreams. We build plans.

And one day, everything we build will be left behind.

But here is the truth that changes everything: The fact that we leave everything behind does not make building meaningless. It makes the building sacred.

It means we must build carefully. It means we must build wisely. It means we must build for something beyond ourselves. It means we must build what lasts—not in stone and mortar, but in lives and hearts.

My parents left everything behind. But they left something in me. And what they left in me continues.

What will you leave? What are you building? What are you investing in? What will remain when you are gone?

These are the questions that matter.

So live with intention. Love with abandon. Serve with joy. Give generously. Build faithfully. And remember: Temporary does not mean meaningless.

When everything we build must eventually be left behind, the only thing that truly matters is what we have built in the hearts of others—and what we have built with God.

"Let us hear the conclusion of the whole matter: Fear God, and keep his commandments: for this is the whole duty of man. For God shall bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether it be good, or whether it be evil." — Ecclesiastes 12:13-14

Prayers

A Prayer for Perspective

Heavenly Father,

When I become overwhelmed by the temporary nature of this life, give me eternal perspective. Help me to see beyond what is seen and to invest in what lasts.

Remind me that my time on earth is precious, and teach me to use it wisely.

As the Psalmist prayed, teach me to number my days, that I may apply my heart unto wisdom.

Help me not to become consumed by things that will eventually pass away. Turn my heart toward the things that have eternal value.

In Jesus' name, Amen.

A Prayer for Grief

Lord Jesus,

I bring my grief to You. The loss I have experienced is real and painful. You wept at the tomb of Lazarus, and You understood my tears.

Help me to grieve with hope. Comfort me with Your presence. And use my loss to draw me closer to You.

You are near to the brokenhearted. Heal my wounds and bind up my heart.

When memories become painful, give me grace. When loneliness comes, remind me that I am not alone.

Teach me to carry my grief without losing my hope in You.

In Jesus' name, Amen.

A Prayer for Legacy

Almighty God,

Show me what truly matters. Help me to build what lasts—faith in the hearts of others, love that endures, and a life that honors You.

Let me not waste my days on things that will fade. Help me to sow seeds that will bear fruit for generations to come.

May my life be a blessing to those who come after me. Let what You have deposited in me become something I faithfully deposit into others.

May the people I encounter become stronger, wiser, and closer to You because I was faithful with the life You gave me.

In Jesus' name, Amen.

A Prayer for Stewardship

Father in Heaven,

Everything I have is from You. Help me to steward Your gifts faithfully—my time, my resources, my relationships, and my influence.

Teach me to hold things loosely and to give generously.

Help me remember that I am not the permanent owner of what You have placed in my hands. I am a steward, and one day I will give an account of how I used what You entrusted to me.

May I use what You have given me for Your glory. Make me a faithful steward of Your manifold grace.

In Jesus' name, Amen.

A Prayer for Hope

Lord of Life,

When I face the reality of death and loss, remind me of the resurrection. You are the resurrection and the life.

Give me hope that transcends the grave. Help me to live with the certainty that death does not have the final word.

You have conquered death, and in You, I have eternal life.

When sorrow overwhelms me, remind me of Your promises. When I think about those I have lost, help me to remember that their earthly departure does not erase the hope I have in You.

Teach me to live today with eternity in my heart.

In Jesus' name, Amen.

A Prayer of Commitment

Lord Jesus Christ,

I give You my life—all of it. My days. My years. My time. My possessions. My resources. My gifts. My relationships. My influence. My legacy.

Help me to live not for myself, but for You. Help me to build not merely for this world, but for eternity. Help me to love not just with words, but in deed and in truth.

Teach me to serve faithfully, give generously, forgive freely, and walk humbly before You.

Let my life point people toward Christ. Let my suffering produce wisdom. Let my experiences become lessons that strengthen others. Let what I have received become something I faithfully pass on.

And when my earthly journey ends, may I have no greater desire than to know that I was faithful with everything You placed in my hands.

May I hear those precious words: "Well done, good and faithful servant."

In Your precious name, Amen.
