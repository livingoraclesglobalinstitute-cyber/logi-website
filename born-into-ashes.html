Born into Ashes: A Theological, Biblical, and Sociological Examination of Childhood Suffering and Inherited Poverty

Prophet Ing. Stephen Adusei
Reading Time: 30 minutes • October 14, 2026

This article examines the theological, biblical, and sociological dimensions of a troubling reality: millions of children suffer from poverty, malnutrition, and limited opportunity through no choice or fault of their own, while others are born into wealth and privilege. Is this asymmetry a matter of chance, divine decree, structural sin, or some combination? Section 1 frames the question, defines key terms, and states the article's formal research question and objectives. Section 2 establishes the methodology and scope of the study. Section 3 surveys the biblical witness on poverty and suffering, with critical exegesis of key passages including Ecclesiastes 9:11, Proverbs 16:33, Exodus 20:5, Ezekiel 18:20, John 9:1–3, and Job 1–2. Section 4 presents major theological interpretations — Augustinian, Reformed, Arminian, Liberationist, Open Theist, and Process — and compares what each can and cannot explain. Section 5 interrogates the concept of "chance" itself, examining biblical vocabulary (miqreh, goral, kata synkyrian) and philosophical views on determinism, compatibilism, and libertarian freedom. Section 6 explores sociological and economic realities, including structural poverty, the myth of meritocracy, the psychology of childhood suffering, verified statistics, and a documented Ghanaian case study. Section 7 outlines the church's response through compassion, justice, advocacy, and structural change. Section 8 addresses philosophical and ethical dimensions, including the problem of evil, the ethics of inequality, the value of suffering, and counterarguments to the article's thesis. Section 9 presents biblical case studies and modern testimonies. Section 10 answers frequently asked theological questions. Section 11 concludes with a limitations section, a clarification on providence, a pastoral word, a call to action, and a closing prayer. The article argues that while birth is not a lottery in the hands of a capricious deity, neither is it a perfectly just system — rather, it is a fallen world where divine providence, human freedom, and structural sin intersect, and where the church is called to embody God's justice and mercy.

Every child enters the world without choosing the family, nation, century, or circumstances into which they are born. Yet the consequences of that unchosen entry are staggering. A child born in a rural village in sub-Saharan Africa may face malnutrition, limited education, and early mortality, while a child born in a Scandinavian city may inherit healthcare, opportunity, and security — neither child earned their starting point. This asymmetry raises one of the most haunting questions in theology and ethics: Is this by chance? Is birth merely a lottery, or does God ordain it? Is poverty a punishment, a test, or a structural evil? And what is the church's responsibility in the face of such inequality? This treatise examines these questions through biblical, theological, sociological, and philosophical lenses, drawing on the ancient Fathers, the Reformers, liberation theologians, and contemporary social theorists (Ecclesiastes 9:11); (Proverbs 16:33).

This article makes a distinctive contribution: it brings biblical theology, competing Christian accounts of divine providence, and the social realities of intergenerational poverty into sustained conversation, and it argues that theological reflection on providence must be accompanied by serious attention to human responsibility, structural injustice, and the church's practical obligations toward suffering children. It does not claim to be the first to make this connection; it seeks to make the connection carefully, transparently, and with due attention to what can and cannot be demonstrated.

1. Framing the Question

The problem of childhood suffering and inherited poverty is not a peripheral theological curiosity; it strikes at the heart of the doctrine of God. If God is good, all-powerful, and just, why does He permit millions of children to suffer through no fault of their own? As Augustine of Hippo wrestled in The City of God, the problem of evil poses a profound challenge to belief in a benevolent Creator (Augustine 1998: 11.18). Yet Augustine also insisted that earthly inequality is temporary and that eternal justice will reverse it.

To engage this question responsibly, we must define our terms. Chance refers to events that appear random or uncaused from a human perspective, yet Scripture acknowledges that "time and chance happen to them all" (Ecclesiastes 9:11 NKJV). Providence refers to God's sovereign ordering of all things, as when Jesus said that not even a sparrow falls without the Father's will (Matthew 10:29-31). Theodicy is the defense of God's goodness in the face of evil. Structural sin describes systems of injustice that transcend individual choices — colonialism, racism, and economic exploitation. Inherited consequence is the biblical principle that actions affect generations (Exodus 20:5); (Ezekiel 18:20).

The question matters because it challenges both the doctrine of a good God and the myth of a fair world. As Jürgen Moltmann wrote in The Crucified God, the question of suffering is not whether God exists, but whether God suffers with us (Moltmann 1974: 200).

"The lot is cast into the lap, But its every decision is from the LORD." — Proverbs 16:33 (NKJV)

2. Research Questions & Objectives

This article investigates the following primary research question:

To what extent can childhood suffering and inherited poverty be understood through biblical teachings on divine providence, human responsibility, and structural injustice?

Five supporting questions guide the analysis:

  1. What does the Bible teach about children who suffer through no fault of their own?
  2. How do different Christian theological traditions explain unequal birth circumstances?
  3. What social and economic factors contribute to intergenerational poverty?
  4. How can the church respond to childhood suffering without blaming the victims?
  5. How should Christians understand divine providence in a world marked by unequal opportunities?

The objective of this study is not to resolve the problem of evil, but to clarify what can and cannot be affirmed theologically, biblically, and sociologically about unequal birth circumstances, and to articulate the church's resulting obligations.

3. Methodology & Scope

This article is a work of interdisciplinary theological analysis. It does not present original empirical fieldwork, clinical data, or survey results. Its methods are fourfold:

  • Biblical interpretation: Key passages are examined in their literary, historical, and canonical contexts, with attention to alternative readings.
  • Theological analysis: Major Christian traditions (Augustinian, Reformed, Arminian, liberationist, open theist, process) are compared on the questions of providence, freedom, and innocent suffering.
  • Literature review: Published theological, philosophical, and sociological scholarship is surveyed and cited in Harvard style.
  • Conceptual analysis: Terms such as chance, providence, responsibility, punishment, consequence, and structural injustice are defined and distinguished.

The article is therefore limited to what theological and interdisciplinary reasoning can establish. It cannot determine why a particular child experiences a particular hardship. It does not replace empirical research on poverty, and its conclusions are shaped by its biblical and Christian theological framework.

4. The Biblical Witness on Poverty & Suffering

Scripture does not shy away from the reality of poverty and suffering. From Genesis to Revelation, the Bible acknowledges that the world is fallen and that inequality is part of the human condition — yet it also insists that God cares deeply for the poor.

A. Old Testament Themes

The Fall introduced toil, scarcity, and thorns into the world (Genesis 3:17-19). Israel's covenant was corporate: blessing or cursing fell on the nation as a whole (Deuteronomy 28). Yet the Law repeatedly commanded care for the poor, the widow, the orphan, and the stranger (Deuteronomy 15:7-11); (Proverbs 31:8-9). The Book of Job dismantles the simplistic view that suffering is always proportional to sin (Job 1:1-22). And Ecclesiastes soberly observes that "time and chance happen to them all" (Ecclesiastes 9:11 NKJV).

B. New Testament Themes

Jesus ministered among the poor and declared, "The poor you will always have with you" (Matthew 26:11 NKJV). The parable of the Rich Man and Lazarus reveals that eternal reversal awaits (Luke 16:19-31). The Beatitudes pronounce blessing on the poor (Luke 6:20-26). James condemns partiality toward the rich (James 2:1-9), and Paul organized a collection for the poor in Jerusalem (2 Corinthians 8:1-15).

C. Critical Exegesis of Key Passages

Collecting proof-texts is not sufficient for an article that addresses a difficult theological question. Each key passage must be interpreted in context, with alternative readings considered.

Ecclesiastes 9:11 — "Time and chance happen to them all." The Hebrew miqreh denotes an occurrence or event, not metaphysical randomness. The Preacher's broader argument (Ecclesiastes 1–12) is about the vanity of human striving and the shared vulnerability of all creatures before death. The verse is a warning against presuming on success, not a claim that the universe is governed by blind chance (Seow 1997: 322).

Proverbs 16:33 — "The lot is cast into the lap." This proverb belongs to wisdom literature's affirmations of God's sovereignty over human decision-making. It does not teach fatalistic determinism; it teaches that even apparently random human procedures fall under God's providential rule. The proverb's context (16:1–33) is about the limits of human planning and the reliability of God's purposes (Waltke 2004: 154).

Exodus 20:5 — "Visiting the iniquity of the fathers." The language is covenant language about the ongoing consequences of idolatry across generations. It is not a doctrine of inherited guilt; the same Law insists that "the fathers shall not be put to death for their children, nor shall the children be put to death for their fathers" (Deuteronomy 24:16 NKJV). The verse describes real social transmission of disadvantage without denying individual responsibility (Fretheim 1991: 228).

Ezekiel 18:20 — "The soul who sins shall die." Ezekiel directly corrects the misuse of the Exodus text by exiles who blamed their ancestors for their suffering. The prophet affirms individual moral responsibility without denying that sin has social consequences. Read together with Exodus 20:5, the two texts hold in tension the reality of transmitted disadvantage and the reality of personal accountability (Block 1997: 578).

John 9:1–3 — The man born blind. Jesus rejects the disciples' retributive assumption that the man's blindness was caused by his own sin or his parents' sin. The disciples' question reflects a common first-century assumption that suffering was proportional to sin. Jesus rejects that assumption directly: "Neither this man nor his parents sinned." He does not, however, offer a general theory of suffering. He redirects attention to the works of God that will be revealed in the man's life. The passage is therefore a warning against using suffering as evidence of divine punishment — a warning that applies especially to children, who cannot be held responsible for their circumstances (Carson 1991: 362).

Job 1–2 — The prose frame. The narrative opens with a heavenly council that the human characters never see. The reader knows Job's suffering is not a punishment; Job and his friends do not. The prose frame denies the retribution principle, the speeches explore its inadequacy, and God's answer (Job 38–41) is presence rather than explanation (Clines 1989: 1–50).

Deuteronomy 28 — Covenant blessing and cursing. This chapter belongs to the covenant renewal ceremonies of ancient Israel. Its blessings and curses describe the corporate consequences of national faithfulness or apostasy under the Sinai covenant. It is not a universal formula for interpreting every individual's circumstances, and the New Testament explicitly relativizes its application to individuals (John 9:1–3); (Luke 13:1–5). Used carefully, Deuteronomy 28 illustrates that sin has real social and generational consequences — but it cannot be applied mechanically to any particular child's suffering.

D. Tension Texts

Scripture contains tensions that resist easy resolution. Proverbs 10:4 suggests laziness leads to poverty, while Proverbs 13:23 observes that injustice destroys the poor (Proverbs 10:4); (Proverbs 13:23). Exodus 20:5 speaks of generational consequence, while Ezekiel 18:20 insists that "the soul who sins shall die" (Exodus 20:5); (Ezekiel 18:20). And Jesus promises provision (Matthew 6:25-34), yet millions of children starve. These tensions call for humility, not glib answers.

5. Theological Interpretations of Inequality

Across church history, theologians have offered differing interpretations of why some are born poor and others rich. This section presents each tradition and then assesses what it can and cannot explain about childhood suffering.

A. Augustinian Perspective

Augustine traced inequality to original sin and its corporate consequences. The city of man is marked by injustice, while the city of God awaits eternal reversal (Augustine 1998: 19.21). What it explains: the universality of sin and the need for redemption. What it struggles to explain: why particular children suffer more than others, if all are equally fallen. Implication for the church: earthly inequality is temporary; the church's task is to embody the justice of the city of God now.

B. Calvinist / Reformed Perspective

John Calvin emphasized God's sovereign decree: "The lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the LORD" (Proverbs 16:33 NKJV). Poverty and wealth are ordained by God for His mysterious purposes (Calvin 1960: 1.16.9). What it explains: God's comprehensive sovereignty and the comfort that nothing is outside His rule. What it struggles to explain: how divine ordination of particular suffering is compatible with God's goodness, and how human responsibility remains meaningful. Implication for the church: the rich are stewards accountable to God; the poor are not outside His purposes.

C. Arminian / Wesleyan Perspective

Arminians distinguish God's perfect will from His permissive will. God grieves with the suffering and calls the church to act. Human free will and sinful social structures create inequality, not divine decree (Wesley 1984: 2.12). What it explains: the reality of human responsibility for suffering, and God's genuine grief over evil. What it struggles to explain: suffering that does not result from identifiable human choices — natural disasters, congenital illness, and the like. Implication for the church: the church cooperates with God's will by relieving suffering and resisting injustice.

D. Liberation Theology

Gustavo Gutiérrez argued that God has a "preferential option for the poor" (Gutiérrez 1973: 195). Poverty is not God's will but a result of sinful systems. The gospel is both spiritual and material liberation. What it explains: the structural and systemic dimensions of poverty, and God's solidarity with the oppressed. What it struggles to explain: suffering that is not caused by identifiable oppression. Implication for the church: the church's mission includes economic and political transformation, not only spiritual conversion.

E. Open Theism

Open theists argue that God does not micromanage every event. Chance and human freedom are real, and God responds to suffering with compassion rather than ordaining it (Boyd 1994: 89). What it explains: the genuineness of human freedom and the reality of contingency. What it struggles to explain: how God can guarantee the ultimate triumph of good if the future is partly open. Implication for the church: the church is God's primary instrument for responding to suffering.

F. Process Theology

Process theologians contend that God is not all-powerful in the classical sense. God lures creation toward good but cannot override free will. Suffering is a byproduct of a world in process (Whitehead 1929: 342). What it explains: why suffering exists in a world of genuine becoming. What it struggles to explain: whether such a God can guarantee final victory over evil — a concern classical theism addresses more directly. Implication for the church: the church participates in God's persuasive work of healing and transformation.

G. Is Poverty a Punishment from God?

The question requires distinguishing at least five categories that are often conflated:

  1. Personal wrongdoing: suffering that follows an individual's own harmful choices (e.g., Proverbs 10:4).
  2. Consequences of others' wrongdoing: children harmed by exploitation, neglect, violence, or irresponsible decisions (e.g., Exodus 20:5; Proverbs 13:23).
  3. Structural injustice: disadvantage perpetuated by social, political, and economic systems (e.g., Isaiah 1:17; Amos 5:11–12).
  4. Natural and circumstantial suffering: illness, disaster, and accident not reducible to moral choice (e.g., Job 1–2; Luke 13:1–5).
  5. Theological interpretations of suffering: discipline, testing, or refinement — which Scripture permits in some cases (Hebrews 12:5–11; 1 Peter 1:6–7) but never as a blanket explanation (John 9:1–3; Luke 13:1–5).

Suffering alone therefore cannot establish that a person has sinned or that God is punishing them. This is the pastoral and theological heart of the article's argument.

6. The Problem of "Chance" — Does It Exist?

Does the Bible allow for chance? The answer is nuanced. Hebrew uses miqreh (happenstance) in Ecclesiastes 9:11 and goral (lot) in Proverbs 16:33. The Greek expression used in Luke 10:31 is kata synkyrian (κατὰ συγκυρίαν), commonly rendered "by chance." This is not the noun tyche, and the verse should not be treated as a lexical occurrence of that term. The significance of the expression for divine providence must be considered within the parable's narrative context: Jesus is describing the failure of religious leaders to respond to a neighbor in need, not making a metaphysical claim about randomness in the universe. The verse illustrates human neglect, not divine indeterminacy (Carson 1991: 362).

Christians have taken different philosophical positions on how divine sovereignty and human responsibility relate. Compatibilism holds that God's sovereign decree and genuine human agency are compatible, and that human beings act freely when they act according to their nature without external coercion. Libertarian freedom, by contrast, holds that genuinely free choices must be undetermined by prior causes, including God's decree. The biblical writers consistently affirm both God's comprehensive rule (Proverbs 16:33); (Ephesians 1:11) and human moral responsibility (Acts 2:23); (Philippians 2:12–13), without explaining in philosophical detail how the two relate. This article treats compatibilism as one historically influential Christian interpretation of that tension, not as the only defensible reading. Readers who hold a libertarian account of freedom will still find the article's central argument — that providence, human responsibility, and structural sin must all be taken seriously — to be coherent within their own framework (Plantinga 1974: 26–29).

Birth is not a lottery in the hands of a capricious deity. No one chooses their parents, country, or century — yet God claims to be sovereign over the womb (Psalm 139:13-16); (Jeremiah 1:5). The blind man in John 9 was not punished for sin but was born blind "that the works of God should be revealed in him" (John 9:1-3 NKJV).

7. Sociological & Economic Realities

Theology must be informed by sociology. Structural poverty is not merely the result of individual choices; it is embedded in systems. Colonialism, globalization, and systemic racism have created intergenerational poverty traps. Unequal access to education, healthcare, and capital perpetuates inequality (Sen 1999: 87).

The myth of meritocracy — the idea that success is purely earned — is challenged by sociology. Birth is the single greatest predictor of life outcomes. As Warren Buffett famously observed, he won the "ovarian lottery" by being born in the right place at the right time (Buffett 1995: 1).

Children internalize poverty as shame. Toxic stress and adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) have long-term cognitive and emotional effects (Felitti et al. 1998: 245). The church must therefore address not only spiritual but also material and psychological dimensions of suffering. It must also acknowledge children's resilience and the role of protective factors such as stable family relationships, community support, and faith communities.

Multidimensional poverty measures deprivation across health, education, and living standards; monetary poverty measures household income below a poverty line. They are not interchangeable, and the rural–urban gap is far sharper under the monetary measure (44.5% vs. 9.8%) than under the multidimensional measure (81.5% vs. 62.5%).

B. A Ghanaian Case Study: Documented Disparities in Childhood Opportunity

Because Living Oracles Global Institute operates within an African context, the argument is grounded in Ghanaian realities. This section distinguishes throughout between documented findings (what the sources report), interpretation (what those findings suggest about inherited poverty), and theological application (how Christian teaching informs the church's response).

Documented findings

The 2019 Multi-Dimensional Child Poverty in Ghana report, produced by Ghana's National Development Planning Commission, the Ghana Statistical Service, and UNICEF, documents the following patterns:

  • Regional disparity: Child poverty is concentrated in the northern regions (Northern, Upper East, Upper West, and parts of Savannah and North East) at rates substantially higher than in Greater Accra and Ashanti.
  • Rural–urban gap: Multidimensional child poverty affects 81.5% of rural children compared with 62.5% of urban children. Monetary child poverty affects 44.5% of rural children compared with 9.8% of urban children — a gap of more than fourfold.
  • Multi-dimensional deprivation: Beyond income, children in the poorest regions face higher rates of stunting, lower school completion, and reduced access to clean water and sanitation.
  • Intergenerational transmission: Parental education and household income strongly predict children's educational attainment and future employment prospects.

Interpretation

These findings cannot be explained solely by individual choices. Children do not choose their region of birth, their household's income level, or whether their community has a functioning clinic. The data show that where a child is born within Ghana — not merely what that child does — substantially shapes access to education, nutrition, and healthcare. This is the empirical form of inherited disadvantage.

Theological application

Read alongside the biblical themes of justice, compassion, human dignity, and communal responsibility (Deuteronomy 15:7-11); (Micah 6:8); (James 1:27), these patterns place a specific obligation on the Ghanaian church. The child in Tamale and the child in Accra are both covenant-bearers. The church's obligation is not abstract charity but concrete engagement with the structural conditions that shape children's lives — through education, healthcare, nutrition, and advocacy.

8. The Church's Response

The early church shared everything (Acts 2:44-45), and James defined pure religion as caring for orphans and widows (James 1:27). Micah 6:8 commands us to "do justly, love mercy, and walk humbly with your God" (Micah 6:8 NKJV). Proverbs 31:8-9 calls us to speak up for the poor (Proverbs 31:8-9). Isaiah 1:17 says, "Learn to do good; seek justice" (Isaiah 1:17 NKJV).

The church's response must include compassion and charity, justice and advocacy, and structural change. Education, healthcare, and economic empowerment can break generational cycles. And prayer and lament must accompany action — for the Psalms of lament give voice to grief (Psalm 13); (Psalm 88), and Jesus Himself wept (John 11:35).

9. Philosophical & Ethical Dimensions

The problem of evil remains the deepest challenge to faith. If God is good and all-powerful, why does evil exist? Moral evil results from human choices; natural evil from the fallenness of creation. The free will defense argues that God permits evil to preserve human freedom (Plantinga 1974: 30). The soul-making theodicy argues that suffering produces character (Hick 1966: 255).

Ethically, we must ask: Is it morally acceptable for some to have much while others have little? John Rawls' "veil of ignorance" invites us to imagine a just society without knowing our place in it (Rawls 1971: 12). Christian ethics answers: Love your neighbor as yourself (Mark 12:31).

Scripture affirms that suffering can produce perseverance, character, and hope (Romans 5:3-5), and that trials produce maturity (James 1:2-4). But this must never be used to justify oppression. As Moltmann insisted, the suffering of the innocent is not a pedagogic tool but a scandal that demands action (Moltmann 1974: 220).

A. Objections Considered

Objection 1 — Strong sovereignty: If God is sovereign, every birth circumstance must be understood as part of God's specific intention. Response: Scripture affirms both God's sovereignty and human responsibility (Philippians 2:12–13); (Acts 2:23). The article argues that sovereignty includes God's permission of secondary causes, not that every instance of suffering is a direct divine decree.

Objection 2 — Purely natural explanation: Unequal birth circumstances are adequately explained by historical, economic, and social causes, without appeal to providence. Response: The article grants the force of this objection for proximate causes while maintaining that theological reflection is still required to answer questions of meaning, obligation, and hope.

Neither objection is dismissed. Both are allowed to qualify the article's claims.

B. Suffering and Future Hope

Pastoral language about hope must be carefully framed. The article distinguishes three things that are often conflated:

  1. Finding meaning or hope while suffering: a legitimate pastoral and theological affirmation (Romans 5:3–5).
  2. Claiming that suffering was necessary or deserved: a claim that Scripture does not support in general (John 9:1–3; Luke 13:1–5).
  3. Working to relieve suffering and prevent avoidable harm: a biblical mandate (Isaiah 1:17; Micah 6:8; James 1:27).

The article does not suggest that suffering is necessarily beneficial, that poverty is spiritually desirable, or that injustice should be accepted passively.

10. Case Studies & Testimonies

Scripture offers case studies of those who suffered greatly yet found God faithful. Job was righteous yet suffered the loss of everything (Job 1:1-22). Joseph was sold into slavery yet later exalted (Genesis 37). David was anointed as a youth yet suffered years of persecution (1 Samuel 16). Jesus was born in a manger, crucified, and exalted (Philippians 2:8-11).

Modern testimonies also matter. Some children rise from poverty; others do not. The role of faith, community, and opportunity cannot be overstated. The church must be a community where the poor are not forgotten and the rich are not idolized (Wolterstorff 1983: 78).

11. Frequently Asked Theological Questions

Q1: Is poverty a punishment from God?
Not always. While Scripture warns that laziness leads to poverty (Proverbs 10:4), it also insists that the righteous may suffer (Job 1:1-22) and that injustice destroys the poor (Proverbs 13:23). Jesus explicitly rejected the idea that the blind man's suffering was due to sin (John 9:1-3).

Q2: Why does God allow some children to starve?
This is the deepest mystery of providence. Scripture affirms that God cares for the poor (Psalm 12:5) and calls His people to act (Isaiah 58:6-7). The answer is not philosophical but practical: God works through His church to feed the hungry (Matthew 25:35-40).

Q3: Does prayer change material circumstances?
Yes — Scripture repeatedly shows prayer moving God to act (James 5:17-18). Yet prayer is not a formula for wealth; it is a relationship of trust (Matthew 6:25-34).

Q4: What is the difference between chance and providence?
Chance describes how events appear from a human perspective; providence describes how God ordains them from His perspective (Proverbs 16:33); (Romans 8:28).

Q5: How should Christians respond to systemic injustice?
With prayer, advocacy, giving, and structural engagement (Micah 6:8); (Isaiah 1:17).

Q6: Will there be poverty in heaven?
No. Revelation 21:4 promises that God will wipe away every tear, and there will be no more death, sorrow, or crying (Revelation 21:4).

Q7: How do we reconcile a good God with innocent suffering?
We do so with humility, lament, and hope. As Moltmann wrote, the crucified God is the answer to the question of innocent suffering — not an explanation, but a presence (Moltmann 1974: 200).

12. Limitations of the Study

This article is a theological and interdisciplinary analysis, not an empirical study of individual children or households. It therefore:

  • Cannot establish why a particular child experiences a particular hardship.
  • Recognizes that Christian traditions differ in their interpretations of providence and suffering.
  • Uses sociological evidence to identify patterns and contributing factors, not to answer every theological question.
  • Presents conclusions that are shaped by its biblical and Christian theological framework.
  • Not every citation and page number has been independently verified at the time of writing. Where verification was not possible, the relevant material has been paraphrased and cited without quotation marks. All direct quotations and publication details should be checked against primary editions before the final version is published.

These limitations are stated not to weaken the argument but to make its claims proportionate to its evidence.

13. Conclusion & Pastoral Application

Inequality is real and deeply troubling. Birth is not a lottery in the hands of a capricious deity, but neither is it a perfectly just system. It is a fallen world where divine providence, human freedom, and structural sin intersect. God's justice is ultimate, even when hidden. The church is called to act, not merely theorize.

A Final Clarification on Providence

This article has argued that God is sovereign, that human beings are morally responsible, and that structural sin is real. It has not claimed that every instance of childhood suffering is directly willed by God, nor that suffering is always punishment for sin, nor that God's permission of an event is equivalent to God's approval of it. Scripture itself distinguishes these categories (John 9:1–3); (Luke 13:1–5); (Acts 2:23). Where the evidence does not permit a conclusion — for example, why one particular child suffers and another does not — this article leaves the question open rather than presuming to answer it. The church's calling is not to explain every instance of suffering but to relieve it, to lament it, and to work for a world in which it is less common.

To the poor: You are not forgotten. God sees you (Psalm 34:6). Your suffering is not meaningless.

To the rich: You are not self-made. Use your wealth for good (1 Timothy 6:17-19).

To the suffering: Lament is allowed. Hope is real (Romans 5:3-5).

Forgive us for indifference and complacency. Teach us to do justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with You. Raise up Your church as a beacon of hope to the poor, a voice for the voiceless, and a refuge for the oppressed. Comfort those who mourn, feed those who hunger, and bring justice to the earth. In the name of Jesus Christ, who became poor that we might be rich, we pray. Amen.”

Prophet Ing. Stephen Adusei

Founder & Lead Lecturer, Living Oracles Global Institute
